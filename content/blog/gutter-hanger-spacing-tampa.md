On a standard seamless aluminum gutter run, hidden hangers go in every 24 inches on center. In corners, valleys, and near downspout outlets, where the gutter carries more weight and takes more wind load, that spacing tightens to every 16 to 18 inches.

That number is not arbitrary. A gutter full of water during a Florida downpour is carrying real weight; a gallon of water weighs about 8.3 pounds, and a 6-inch or 7-inch seamless aluminum gutter running the length of a typical roofline can hold dozens of gallons at once. Space the hangers too far apart and the gutter sags between them, water pools instead of draining toward the downspout, and the fascia takes on load it was not built to carry over time.

**Hidden hangers vs. the old spike-and-ferrule system**

Most Tampa Bay homes built before the 2000s were installed with spikes and ferrules: a long nail driven through the gutter and into the fascia board, with a metal sleeve keeping the gutter from crushing. It works for a while, but Florida's humidity swings put the fascia lumber through constant expansion and contraction, and spikes back out of that movement faster than almost any other climate in the country. Once a few spikes work loose, the whole run starts to sag.

Hidden hangers solve that differently. Instead of a nail through the face of the gutter, a hanger clips onto the inside lip and screws directly into the fascia, spreading the load across a wider bracket instead of a single point. It holds tighter through humidity cycles and it is what JR One installs on every gutter job, new construction or replacement.

**Why spacing changes by gutter size and location**

A 6-inch gutter and a 7-inch gutter do not carry the same water volume, so they do not get the same hanger spacing treatment. JR One installs 6-inch and 7-inch seamless aluminum systems only, sized to the roof's square footage and expected rainfall load, and hanger spacing gets tightened on the larger 7-inch runs and anywhere water volume spikes, meaning corners, valleys, and the last few feet before a downspout.

Gutter guards do not change this math. A guard keeps leaf debris out of the channel, but the water still has to move through the same gutter at the same volume during a storm, so hanger spacing is set by rainfall and roof area, not by whether a guard is installed on top.

**New construction vs. re-hanging an existing run**

On a new build, hanger spacing gets set once during install and does not need revisiting unless the fascia itself is replaced later. On an existing home, the more common call is a re-hang: the gutters are structurally fine, but the fascia has softened in a section, spikes have started backing out, or a prior contractor spaced the hangers too far apart to save material on the job. A re-hang on hidden hangers, spaced correctly the first time, is a fraction of the cost of waiting until a full section pulls away from the house and takes fascia with it.

**Why this is not a DIY fix**

Re-hanging a gutter section looks simple from the ground, but it means working off a ladder at roofline height, removing wet gutter sections that can be heavier than they look, and confirming the fascia underneath is solid enough to hold new fasteners before anything gets reattached. If the fascia has already softened, adding new hangers into rotted wood just repeats the same failure a few months later. That is the part a homeowner usually cannot see from the ground, and it is the reason a quick hanger fix sometimes turns into a fascia conversation.

**What to check on your own gutters**

- Look along the run for visible sag between hangers; a straight, level line means the spacing is holding.
- Check whether you have hidden hangers or the older spike-and-ferrule style; spikes backing out even slightly is worth a call before it turns into a full detach.
- Pay attention to corners and the last stretch before each downspout; these spots should have tighter spacing than the straight runs.
- After a hard storm, walk the gutter line and look for standing water pooling in one section instead of draining; that is often a spacing or pitch problem, not a clog.
